------------------------------------------------------------------------------   Introduction   ------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Survival analysis is a statistical framework for studying the timing of events and their evolution over an observation period. It focuses on outcomes that unfold over time, where the event of interest may vary depending on the scientific context. A central challenge in this setting is that event times are often not fully observed, necessitating methods that appropriately incorporate incomplete follow-up information. This motivates a general emphasis on structuring time-to-event data in a manner that remains valid under varying patterns of observation and censoring. The primary quantities of interest characterize how event occurrence accumulates over time and how the associated risk evolves dynamically as individuals remain under observation. These quantities provide complementary perspectives on the evolution of survival experiences across populations and over time. Building on this foundation, survival analysis supports both descriptive summaries and modeling approaches that relate time-to-event behavior to explanatory factors, while relying on assumptions regarding the relationship between the observation process, censoring, and the underlying event-generating mechanism \citep{klein2003survival,kleinbaum1996survival}.

The Kaplan--Meier estimator is a widely used non-parametric method for describing survival patterns in time-to-event data subject to censoring \citep{kaplan1958nonparametric}. It constructs an empirical survival function by sequentially updating survival probabilities at observed event times, resulting in a stepwise representation of the survival trajectory over time. This estimator naturally accommodates incomplete follow-up while maintaining an interpretable characterization of changes in survival experience across ordered event times. To compare survival patterns between groups, rank-based procedures are commonly employed to assess differences in event occurrences over time. These methods evaluate discrepancies between observed and expected event patterns under a common survival structure, providing a basis for assessing whether groups exhibit systematically different survival behavior \citep{mantel1966evaluation}. Extensions of these comparison procedures allow for alternative weighting schemes and adjustments for additional data structure, enabling more flexible comparisons across diverse survival settings \citep{harrington1982class}.

The Cox proportional hazards model is a fundamental framework in survival analysis for examining the relationship between covariates and time-to-event outcomes \citep{cox1972regression}. Its semi-parametric structure allows for flexible modeling of survival processes while simultaneously adjusting for multiple explanatory variables. By focusing on relative hazard comparisons, the model provides a widely used approach for quantifying covariate effects through hazard ratios and adjusted survival estimates. Estimation is conducted using likelihood-based procedures that appropriately accommodate right-censored observations through the construction of time-evolving risk sets \citep{cox1972regression}. A central component of the framework is the proportional hazards assumption, which implies constancy of relative risks over the follow-up period. The model is also adaptable to a range of study designs and time scales, including settings with delayed entry, dynamic populations, and alternative definitions of the underlying survival timeline \citep{andersen1982cox}.

Causal survival analysis is concerned with understanding how time-to-event outcomes would differ under alternative treatment assignments at the population level. The framework is based on counterfactual reasoning to compare hypothetical survival experiences, acknowledging that only one potential outcome is observed for each individual \citep{rubin1974estimating,hernan2020whatif}. Identification of causal effects relies on key structural assumptions, including consistency, conditional exchangeability, and positivity, which link observed data to the underlying potential outcomes \citep{robins1986new,hernan2020whatif}. Graphical causal structures (DAGs) are often used to represent confounding mechanisms and potential sources of informative censoring that may bias naive survival comparisons \citep{greenland1999causal,pearl2009causality}. Within this setting, marginal survival quantities and corresponding contrasts are the primary targets of inference. Estimation is typically achieved through standardization and inverse probability weighting strategies that construct pseudo-populations in which treatment groups are balanced with respect to observed covariates \citep{robins2000marginal,austin2011introduction}. Marginal structural survival models extend these ideas by combining weighted estimation with hazard-based modeling frameworks to estimate causal treatment effects in the presence of measured time-dependent confounding \citep{robins2000marginal}. In practice, their implementation requires careful evaluation of covariate balance, weight stability, and the plausibility of the underlying causal assumptions \citep{austin2011introduction,hernan2020whatif}.

Case-base sampling provides a flexible framework for modeling time-to-event processes through the comparison of observed events with sampled time points across the follow-up period \citep{bhatnagar2020casebase}. By representing the study timeline through discrete person-time contributions, the approach facilitates the construction of a reference structure against which event occurrences can be evaluated \citep{bhatnagar2020casebase}. This formulation naturally incorporates time within the modeling process, thereby allowing a broad class of hazard functions to be represented within a unified framework \citep{bhatnagar2020casebase}. The methodology accommodates both parametric and semi-parametric specifications, including smooth functional forms that capture temporal variation in risk \citep{hanley2009fitting,bhatnagar2020casebase}. Through spline-based extensions, the framework can additionally be used to derive cumulative incidence measures and related risk summaries over time \citep{hanley2009fitting,bhatnagar2020casebase}. Comparisons with conventional survival modeling approaches suggest that case-base sampling can produce smooth and interpretable risk trajectories while maintaining close agreement with established methods \citep{bhatnagar2020casebase}.

------------------------------------------------------------------------------   Assumptions   -------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The non-informative censoring assumption states that the censoring mechanism provides no additional information about the remaining event time beyond that already captured by the observed treatment and covariates. In other words, conditional on $\X$ and $A$, the censoring process is independent of the potential outcome (or event time). Formally, this assumption is expressed as $T^{*} \perp C \mid \X, A$ \citep{robins1986new,hernan2020whatif}. This condition ensures that, after conditioning on $(A,\X)$, censoring does not introduce bias in the estimation of survival or hazard-related quantities \citep{hernan2020whatif,klein2003survival}.

Consistency (SUTVA) states that the observed outcome for individual $i$ under the received treatment equals the corresponding potential outcome under that treatment, formally $T_i^* = T_i^*(A_i)$ \citep{rubin1974estimating,hernan2020whatif}. Consistency can be decomposed into two key conditions. The first is no interference, which requires that the potential outcome of unit $i$ depends only on its own treatment assignment and is unaffected by the treatment assignments of other units $j \neq i$. The second is the well-defined treatment condition, which requires that each treatment level corresponds to a single, unambiguous intervention; violations arise when heterogeneity in implementation renders $T^*(1)$ not uniquely defined \citep{hernan2020whatif}. Under SUTVA and appropriate adjustment for confounding, this framework implies an equivalence between conditional and potential outcome distributions within treatment strata, namely $\mathbb{P}\left(T^*(a) > t\right) = \mathbb{P}\left(T^* > t \mid A = a\right),$ within the stratum $\{A=a\}$ \citep{hernan2020whatif}.

Conditional exchangeability states that, given a set of observed covariates $\X$, the treatment assignment provides no additional information about the potential outcomes. In other words, treatment assignment is ``as good as random'' within strata defined by $\X$ \citep{rubin1974estimating,hernan2020whatif}. Formally, this assumption is expressed as $T^{*}(a) \perp A \mid \X,\ \forall\, a \in \{0,1\}$. A key practical implication of this assumption is that all confounders of the relationship between $A$ and $T^{*}$ must be observed and included in $\X$. Under this condition, adjustment for $\X$ is sufficient to eliminate confounding bias in the estimation of treatment effects \citep{hernan2020whatif}.

The positivity assumption requires that every covariate profile $\X = \X$ has a strictly positive probability of receiving each treatment level. Intuitively, this ensures that no subgroup defined by covariates is deterministically assigned to or excluded from a particular treatment. Formally, this condition is expressed as $0 < \mathbb{P}(A = a \mid \X = \X) < 1,\ \forall\, a \in \{0,1\}$ \citep{robins1986new,hernan2020whatif}. This assumption guarantees that treatment and control groups are adequately represented across all covariate strata, thereby enabling meaningful comparisons and identification of causal effects within each stratum \citep{hernan2020whatif}.

A correctly specified propensity score model is one in which the estimated propensity score consistently recovers the true treatment assignment mechanism. Let $\hat{e}(\X)$ denote the estimated propensity score and let $e(\X) = \mathbb{P}(A=1 \mid \X)$ denote the true propensity score. The assumption of correct specification implies that $\hat{e}(\X) \xrightarrow{p} \mathbb{P}(A=1 \mid \X)$ as the sample size increases \citep{hernan2020whatif,austin2011introduction}. This condition ensures that the estimated propensity score is a consistent estimator of the true conditional probability of receiving treatment given covariates, thereby enabling valid adjustment for confounding in subsequent causal inference procedures \citep{austin2011introduction,hernan2020whatif}.

Non-informative censoring, or a correctly specified inverse probability of censoring weight (IPCW) model, allows for unbiased estimation of the target estimand by appropriately accounting for the censoring mechanism. In particular, under the assumption of non-informative censoring, or when the censoring process is correctly specified, IPCW reweights the observed uncensored individuals to represent the full population that would have been observed in the absence of censoring \citep{robins1986new,hernan2020whatif}. This weighting scheme enables consistent estimation of functionals of $T^{*}$ despite the presence of censoring \citep{hernan2020whatif}.

A correctly specified marginal structural Cox model (MS-Cox model) assumes that, at the population level, the hazard function is proportional in the treatment variable. This implies that the effect of treatment on the hazard is multiplicative and constant over time on the hazard ratio scale \citep{robins2000marginal,hernan2020whatif}. Consequently, the causal effect of treatment can be represented within a proportional hazards framework in the marginal (population-level) setting \citep{robins2000marginal}.

Table \ref{tab:sim_rmst_s1} summarizes the finite-sample performance of the marginal structural Cox (MS-Cox) and inverse probability weighted (IPW) case-base estimators for the causal RMST difference under the null treatment effect scenario, evaluated at fixed horizons $\tau \in \{1,2,3,4\}$.. For both methods, the mean estimated RMST difference remains close to the true value of zero at each evaluation horizon, although the estimates become slightly more negative as $\tau$ increases. The absolute bias increases gradually with $\tau$, reflecting the greater difficulty of estimating RMST at longer follow-up times, while the accompanying Monte Carlo standard errors also increase because of the larger sampling variability. The empirical standard deviation, mean estimated standard error, and RMSE all increase monotonically with $\tau$, consistent with the reduction in effective sample size at later time points. For the MS-Cox estimator, the empirical coverage remains stable and close to the nominal $95\%$ level across all horizons, ranging from 0.966 to 0.970. In contrast, the case-base estimator exhibits comparable coverage for shorter horizons but shows a noticeable decline at $\tau = 4$, where coverage decreases to 0.892, indicating some underestimation of uncertainty at longer follow-up times. Despite this difference in interval coverage, the two approaches produce nearly identical RMST point estimates, biases, empirical standard deviations, estimated standard errors, and RMSE values across all evaluation horizons, demonstrating essentially equivalent estimation performance for the causal RMST difference.

Table \ref{tab:sim_rmst_s2} summarizes the finite-sample performance of the marginal structural Cox (MS-Cox) and inverse probability weighted (IPW) case-base estimators for the causal RMST difference under the strong treatment effect scenario. For both methods, the mean estimated RMST difference closely follows the true RMST difference, becoming increasingly negative as the evaluation horizon $\tau$ increases. The magnitude of the bias also increases with $\tau$ for both estimators, although the MS-Cox estimator consistently exhibits smaller bias than the case-base estimator at each time horizon. As expected, the empirical standard deviation, mean estimated standard error, and RMSE all increase monotonically with $\tau$, reflecting the greater uncertainty associated with estimating RMST differences at longer follow-up times. For the MS-Cox estimator, empirical coverage remains close to the nominal $95\%$ level across all horizons, decreasing only slightly from 0.985 at $\tau=1$ to 0.939 at $\tau=4$. In contrast, the case-base estimator demonstrates substantially lower coverage throughout, ranging from 0.876 at $\tau=1$ to 0.810 at $\tau=2$, before increasing modestly to 0.892 at $\tau=4$, indicating persistent undercoverage despite the increase in reported standard errors at longer horizons. Overall, both methods display similar patterns in variability and precision as $\tau$ increases; however, under a strong treatment effect the MS-Cox estimator yields consistently smaller bias and more reliable confidence interval coverage than the case-base estimator, leading to improved finite-sample performance.

As fewer individuals remain under observation at later time points, the amount of information available for estimation decreases, leading to greater variability in the estimated hazard ratio. This effect is particularly pronounced towards the tail of the follow-up period, where the risk set becomes small and estimates are consequently more sensitive to stochastic fluctuations.
